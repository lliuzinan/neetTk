1. My conclusion

For the stated Aa x Aa model, the probability of aa is 1/4. In 40 offspring, the expected aa count is 10; it is not a promise of exactly 10 in every batch.

Why this field matters: State the prediction and its limits, not just a ratio.

2. Evidence and source

This is a constructed learning example, not an experiment. Both parents are Aa. The MedQGo Mendelian guide explains the gamete combinations. A fictional observed batch contains 12 AA, 17 Aa and 11 aa. These numbers are invented for comparison with the prediction.

Why this field matters: Separate the given model, the reference and the invented observations.

3. Assumptions

At this autosomal locus, each parent contributes A or a with probability 1/2; fertilisation is random. To compare predicted genotype proportions with surviving offspring, assume equal survival and reliable scoring. Complete dominance is needed for the phenotype grouping, not for the 1:2:1 genotype calculation.

Why this field matters: Name the conditions your calculation uses.

4. Reasoning or calculation

The four ordered pairings are A+A, A+a, a+A and a+a, each with probability 1/4. The two middle pairings give Aa, so genotype probabilities are 1/4 AA, 1/2 Aa and 1/4 aa. Multiplying by 40 gives 10, 20 and 10 expected offspring. Under complete dominance, AA and Aa share the dominant phenotype: expected totals are 30 and 10. The invented observed totals are 12+17=29 dominant and 11 recessive.

Why this field matters: Show the route from gametes to genotypes before grouping phenotypes.

5. Limits and uncertainties

The invented batch does not establish a biological result. An observed 11 aa instead of an expected 10 does not by itself disprove segregation or prove all assumptions. A statistical assessment would require an appropriate analysis. Dominant appearance alone cannot distinguish AA from Aa.

Why this field matters: Do not turn a small discrepancy or an appearance into proof.
